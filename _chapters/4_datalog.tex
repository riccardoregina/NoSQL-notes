\chapter{Datalog: Reasoning on Relational Databases}

Although Datalog is a query language that applies to relational databases and relational databases are traditionally associated with SQL, Datalog is fundamentally \emph{not} SQL. 
It belongs to the family of logic-based query languages and, as such, shares philosophical ground with rule-based reasoning systems. 

\section{Origins and Motivations}

Datalog emerged in the late 1970s and early 1980s from research on \keyword{deductive databases}, a field that combined relational database theory with logic programming. 
The key idea behind deductive databases is to extend a traditional relational database with a set of \emph{rules} that allow the system to \emph{deduce} new facts from explicitly stored ones. 

Datalog represents a completely different way of querying a relational database: it is a subset of Prolog, an exponent of the \emph{logic programming paradigm}, and crucially allows recursion, which is not supported by standard SQL.

Consider a database with two tables:

\vspace{0.5cm}
\begin{minipage}{0.45\textwidth}
    \centering
    \textbf{Flight}
    \begin{tabular}{ccc}
        \toprule
        \textit{Origin} & \textit{Destination} & \textit{Airline} \\
        \midrule
        LHR & GLA & BA \\
        LHR & EDI & EZ \\
        GLA & JFK & BA \\
        EDI & GLA & EZ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{minipage}
\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.45\textwidth}
    \centering
    \textbf{Airport}
    \begin{tabular}{cc}
        \toprule
        \textit{Code} & \textit{City} \\
        \midrule
        LHR & London \\
        GLA & Glasgow \\
        EDI & Edinburgh \\
        JFK & New York \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{minipage}
\vspace{0.5cm}

\vspace{3mm}
Say we want to find which airlines fly direct from London to Glasgow.
The SQL query would be:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=sql]
SELECT F.airline
FROM Flight AS F,
     Airport AS A1,
     Airport AS A2
WHERE A1.city = 'London'
AND   A2.city = 'Glasgow'
AND   A1.code = F.origin
AND   A2.code = F.destination
\end{lstlisting}
while the First-Order Logic (FOL) representation of this query is simply:
\[
    \set{Z \mid \exists X,Y:\; \text{airport}(X,\text{London}) \land \text{airport}(Y,\text{Glasgow}) \land \text{flight}(X,Y,Z)}
\]
Notice how the FOL formula is much more expressive and succinct than the SQL query: it directly captures the \emph{logical structure} of the request without the syntactic overhead of joins, aliases, and variable naming conventions.

Let's consider another query: \q{Is it possible to reach Glasgow from London by taking flights (with any number of connections)?}

Neither FOL nor standard SQL can express such a request for an \emph{arbitrary} graph, because the number of intermediate stops is not known in advance. Datalog, however, can express this with a \emph{recursive rule}:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=prolog]
reachable(X,Y) :- flight(X,Y,Z).
reachable(X,Y) :- flight(X,W,Z), reachable(W,Y).

ans(X,Y) :- reachable(X,Y), 
            airport(X, 'London'), 
            airport(Y, 'Glasgow').
\end{lstlisting}

The first rule states that \(Y\) is reachable from \(X\) if there is a direct flight (base case). The second rule states that \(Y\) is reachable from \(X\) if there is a flight to some intermediate \(W\), and \(Y\) is reachable from \(W\) (recursive case).

Unlike FOL and SQL, Datalog can express the \keyword{transitive closure} of a graph\footnote{In a directed graph \(\langle V,E\rangle\), the transitive closure is the set of all pairs of nodes \((x,y)\) such that there is a path of length \(1\) or more from \(x\) to \(y\).}, which is necessary for reachability, because it extends FOL with the \keyword{least fixed-point operator} (LFP). For example,
\[
[\text{LFP}\; R(x,y) = E(x,y) \lor \exists z\, (E(x,z) \land R(z,y))]
\]
expresses that \(R\) is the smallest relation such that one can go from \(x\) to \(y\) directly via an edge \(E(x,y)\), or go from \(x\) to some \(z\) via an edge \(E(x,z)\) and then from \(z\) to \(y\) via \(R\), which is exactly the reachability relation.

\section{Syntax and Semantics}

Datalog is a subset of the \emph{Prolog} programming language and, because of this, inherits its syntax. However, Datalog imposes restrictions (e.g., no function symbols) to guarantee termination and efficient evaluation over finite databases.

\subsection{Terminology: Constants, Variables, Terms, Atoms}
We now present and formally describe the fundamental building blocks of Datalog.

\begin{definition}{Constant}
    A \keyword{constant} is a specific database value. Constants are written in lowercase or enclosed in single quotes. 
\end{definition}
Examples of constants of the previous example database are \code{'London'}, \code{'BA'}, \code{42}.
Constants serve as the \q{nouns} of the language, denoting known entities, strings, or numbers.

\begin{definition}{Variable}
    A \keyword{variable} denotes an unknown value to be matched during query evaluation. Variables are written with an initial uppercase letter or underscore.
\end{definition}
For example, \code{X}, \code{Origin}, \code{\_x}, and \code{\_} are all syntactically valid variables. The anonymous variable \code{\_} is a special placeholder that matches any value and immediately discards the binding---it is used when the matched value is irrelevant to the rest of the rule and need not be referenced elsewhere.

\begin{definition}{Term}
    A \keyword{term} is either a \emph{constant} or a \emph{variable}.
\end{definition}
Unlike full Prolog, Datalog \emph{does not} allow function symbols (complex terms like \code{f(X, Y)} or \code{[X|Y]}). 
This restriction ensures that the set of all possible values that can appear in a derivation is exactly the finite set of constants occurring in the database.
This property is known as the \keyword{finite domain assumption}. 
Because no new compound terms can be constructed during rule evaluation, the search space remains bounded and termination is guaranteed.

\begin{definition}{Atom}
    An \keyword{atom} is a predicate symbol applied to a tuple of terms: \(p(t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n)\). Atoms represent \emph{relationships} between terms.
\end{definition}
For example, the atom \code{flight(X, Y, Z)} asserts that a relation named \code{flight} holds among the terms \code{X}, \code{Y}, and \code{Z}. The \textbf{arity} (number of arguments) of an atom is fixed by the relation schema: \code{flight} has arity 3, \code{airport} has arity 2.

Atoms are categorized according to whether they refer to stored or derived relations:

\begin{definition}{EDB Atom}
    An \keyword{Extensional Database Atom} is relation that is explicitly stored in the database (e.g., \code{flight}, \code{airport}).
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{IDB Atom}
    An \keyword{Intensional Database Atom} is a relation that is derived by defined rules (e.g., \code{reachable}, \code{ans}).
\end{definition}

Intuitively, EDB atoms are ground truths, while IDB atoms must be derived and are latent information in the database.

\subsection{Rules and Queries}

A Datalog program consists of a finite set of \keyword{rules} and a \keyword{query}.

\begin{definition}{Datalog Rule}
    A \keyword{Datalog rule} has the form:
    \[
    \text{head} \;\text{\texttt{:-}}\; \text{atom}_1, \text{atom}_2, \dots, \text{atom}_n .
    \]
    where the \emph{head} is a single IDB atom (the conclusion being derived) and comma-separated \emph{atoms} (EDB or IDB) are evaluated as in conjuction.
\end{definition}
A Datalog rule specifies that if the body holds, then also the head does.

\begin{definition}{Fact}
    A \keyword{fact} is a rule with an empty body (written simply as \code{head.}) and represents a ground truth.
\end{definition}
A fact is essentially an EDB atom.

\begin{definition}{Datalog Query}
    A \keyword{Datalog query} is a special rule where the head is a distinguished predicate (often \code{ans} or \code{query}) containing the variables to be returned. 
    The answer to the query is the set of all tuples that make the body true.
\end{definition}

To guarantee that the result of any query is finite and computable, Datalog rules must be \keyword{safe}.

\begin{definition}{Rule Safety}
    A Datalog rule is \keyword{safe} if \emph{every} variable appearing in the head of the rule also appears in at least one positive (non-negated) body atom. 
\end{definition}
In logic programming, variables are implicitly drawn from the entire universe of discourse (e.g., all possible text strings, all possible integers). 
A positive atom like \texttt{airport(X, 'London')} acts as a filter, restricting \(X\) to only the values actually present in the database.
Consider the \emph{unsafe} rule \code{ans(X) :- not flight(X,Y,Z)}. 
In this rule, the variable \(X\) is only evaluated against a negative condition and hence the query engine would attempt to generate an output for every single possible string.
So, there are infinitely many values for \(X\), \(Y\), and \(Z\) that do not exist in the finite flight table.

\subsection{Semantics: Immediate Consequence and Least Fixed Point}

Rules of a Datalog program are logical propositions asserting a property of the output.
Hence, we can convert a Datalog program \(P\) to a set of universally quantified FOL sentences \(\Sigma[P]\) that describe the desired output of \(P\) on the database \(D\).
For example, the rule \(\rho\)
\[
    r_0 \;\text{\texttt{:-}}\; r_1, r_2, \dots, r_n .
\]
is converted to \(\phi[\rho]\)
\[
    \forall V_1, V_2, \ldots V_n (r_1 \land r_2 \land \ldots \land r_n \to r_0)
\]
where the variables \(V_i\) are the variables occurring in \(\rho\). 

\begin{definition}{Model}
    A database\footnote{A database here is intended as a set of facts, which does not necessary equal the actual original database.} \(D\) is a \keyword{model} of a sentence \(\phi[\rho]\) if whenever there exists a homomorphism \(h\) from the body atoms of \(\phi[\rho]\) to \(D\) then \(h\) is also a homomorphism from the conclusion of \(\phi[\rho]\) to \(D\).
\end{definition}
The homomorphism formalizes the concept of pattern matching of a rule with some atom of the database. The definition is telling us that if \(D\models\phi[\rho]\), then if \(r_1,r_2 \ldots r_n \subset D\), we also have \(r_0 \subset D\).
Let's see how to define such a homomorphism.

\begin{definition}{Substitution}
    A \keyword{substitution} is a mapping \(\sigma: A \to B\) from a set of terms\footnote{Recall that \q{term} is the general name to refer to both variables and constants.} \(A\) to another set of terms \(B\).
\end{definition}
We can now extend the concept of substitution to set of atoms by finally defining our homomorphism \(h\), that is namely a homomorphism between two set of atoms \(A\) and \(B\) that is based on the \(\sigma\) substitution:
\[
    r(t_1,\ldots,t_n) \in A \implies r(t_1,\ldots,t_n)\sigma = r(\sigma(t_1),\ldots,\sigma(t_n)) \in B.
\]
However, such homomorphism may be infinite and so, given a database and a sentence \(\phi[\rho]\), infinite models may exist.
We are interested in the \keyword{minimal model} though. Such minimal model is unique because Datalog rules are Horn clauses, which possess the \emph{model intersection property}: the intersection of any set of models is still a model. 
Therefore, intersecting all valid models yields a single, smallest model containing only the facts logically necessitated by the program.
This unique model is obtained by applying a fixpoint procedure, that we will see in a moment.

The semantics of a Datalog program is defined in terms of the \keyword{immediate consequence operator} \(T_P\), which formalizes the idea of \q{applying all rules once} to a given database.

\begin{definition}{Immediate Consequence Operator}
    Given a Datalog program \(P\) and a database \(D\), the \keyword{immediate consequence operator} \(T_P(D)\) is the set of all facts that can be derived by applying the rules of \(P\) to \(D\) in a single step. Formally:
    \[
    T_P(D) = \{\, \text{head}\sigma \mid (\text{head} \;\text{\texttt{:-}}\; B_1, \dots, B_n) \in P \text{ and } \{B_1\sigma, \dots, B_n\sigma\} \subseteq D \,\}
    \]
    where \(\sigma\) is the preoviously mentioned homomorphism and maps all variables to constants.
    In words, if there is a rule in \(D\) s.t. by replacing (via \(\sigma\)) its variables with constants its body atoms \(B_1,\ldots,B_n\) belong to \(D\), then we add the rule head to \(D\) with the applied substitutions.
\end{definition}

Starting from the EDB atoms of the database \(D\), repeated application of \(T_P\) builds larger and larger databases:
\[
T_{P,0}(D) = D, \quad T_{P, i+1}(D) = T_{P, i}(D) \cup T_P(T_{P, i}(D))
\]
Since Datalog rules are \keyword{monotonic} (adding facts only adds more derivable facts, never removes them), the starting database is finite and no datalog rules can create new constants (this is why functions are not allowed), this sequence converges after finitely many steps to a \keyword{least fixed point}, denoted \[T_{P,\infty}(D)=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}T_{P,i}(D).\]

This fixed point is exactly the set of all facts logically entailed by the program and the database: it is the \keyword{minimal model} of the program.

Let's see immediate consequence \q{in action} in the following example.

\begin{example}{}
    Consider the program:
    \begin{lstlisting}[language=prolog]
flight(lhr, gla, ba).
flight(gla, jfk, ba).

reachable(X,Y) :- flight(X,Y,_).
reachable(X,Y) :- flight(X,W,_), reachable(W,Y).
    \end{lstlisting}

    We start from the initial situation 
    \[T_{P,0}(D) = D = \set{\text{flight(lhr,gla,ba)},\; \text{flight(gla,jfk,ba)}}\]
    i.e. the EDB atoms of the database \(D\).
    Then, applying \(T_P\) another time, we obtain 
    \[T_{P,1}(D) = T_{P,0} \cup \set{\text{reachable(lhr,gla)},\; \text{reachable(gla,jfk)}}\]
    since a single application of \(T_P\) adds the facts that can be entailed applying a single rule (computation) step. 
    Let's apply \(T_P\) again:
    \[T_{P,2}(D) = T_{P,1}(D) \cup \set{\text{reachable(lhr,jfk)}}\]
    where \code{reachable(lhr,jfk)} was derived by applying the recursive case of the \code{reachable} rule because we already had \code{flight(lhr,gla,ba)} and \code{reachable(gla,jfk)}.
    Applying another time \(T_P\), we understand that the fixed point is reached, since we cannot add new facts:
    \[T_{P,3}(D) = T_{P,2}(D)\]

    The least fixed point contains exactly the direct and indirect reachability pairs.
\end{example}

\begin{example}{Performance Concerns}
    Since reachability (and, in general, transitive closure of some relation) is by far the most common query, it is useful to consider some performance concerns.
    Suppose we have to find all the reachable airports from the \code{lhr} airport.
    We could first compute the transitive closure of the \code{flight} predicate and then select only the inferred \code{reachable} facts with \code{lhr} as the first constant:
    \begin{lstlisting}[language=prolog]
reachable(X, Y) :- flight(X, Y, _).
reachable(X, Y) :- reachable(X, Z), flight(Z, Y, _).

ans(Y) :- reachable(lhr, Y).
    \end{lstlisting}
    but, by doing this, we would compute the whole transitive closure only to select very few of its results.
    Another, more efficient way of making this query is by binding the constant early:
    \begin{lstlisting}[language=prolog]
reachable_from_lhr(Y) :- flight(lhr, Y, _).
reachable_from_lhr(Y) :- reachable_from_lhr(Z), flight(Z, Y, _).

ans(Y) :- reachable_from_lhr(Y).
    \end{lstlisting}
\end{example}

\section{Limitations and Capabilities of Datalog}
Monotonicity is the golden property that guarantees termination in Datalog. However, it comes with some limitations.
Suppose we have a database containing information about flights s.t. \code{flight(lhr, gla, ba)} means that there is a flight, operated by \code{ba}, from \code{lhr} to \code{gla}:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=prolog]
flight(lhr, gla, ba).
flight(gla, jfk, ba).
\end{lstlisting}
Now, we want to define a predicate \code{at\_most\_one(X)} to find all the airports that have at most one departing flight. 
Let's assume that this predicate exists so that the immediate consequence operator adds \code{at\_most\_one(lhr)} and \code{at\_most\_one(gla)} to the database.
But, if a new fact is added (say \code{flight(lhr, lgw, ba)}) s.t. the database becomes
\begin{lstlisting}[language=prolog]
flight(lhr, gla, ba).
flight(gla, jfk, ba).
at_most_one(lhr).
at_most_one(gla).
flight(lhr, lgw, ba).
\end{lstlisting}
And so the previous result (\code{at\_most\_one(lhr)}) is now invalid, but the monotonicity property states that facts can only be added and so this predicate \emph{cannot} be expressed.
Similarly, we \emph{cannot} express the predicate \code{no\_flights(X)}: adding a fact would invalidate the results. Moreover, this last predicate would be unsafe:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=prolog]
no_flights(X) :- not flight(X, _, _).
\end{lstlisting}

In contrast, we \emph{can} express the \code{at\_least\_one(X)} predicate: adding new facts will not invalidate the result.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=prolog]
at_least_one(X) :- flight(X, _, _).
\end{lstlisting}

Finally, we \emph{cannot} express the \code{exactly\_one(X)} predicate that would be defined as
\begin{lstlisting}[language=prolog]
exactly_one(X) :- at_least_one(X), at_most_one(X).
\end{lstlisting}
but we cannot express \code{at\_most\_one(X)}.

Another Datalog limitation is that it \emph{cannot} track the nodes of a path, it only creates new facts.
For example, we cannot express a query like \q{find the couples of airports X, Y s.t. one can travel from X to Y without touching the same airport twice}.

These limitations should not hide how many queries can actually be expressed with Datalog: we \emph{can} make basically every other query that does not require involve the previous, illegal ones.
For example, we \emph{can} express the query \q{find the couples of airports X, Y s.t. one can reach Y from X with flights covered by exactly two airlines}:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=prolog]
% The idea is to make a path "one_airline" as long as it is covered by only one airline (we save the name of the airline in the newly generated fact to simulate a memory) and then make it "two_airlines" as soon as we extend it with a flight of a different airline (which will be saved together with the other airline). Once a path is "two_airlines", it can be extended with whatever flight of one of the two companies.
one_airline(X, Y, C) :- flight(X, Y, C). % Base case
one_airline(X, Y, C) :- one_airline(X, Z, C), flight(Z, Y, C). % Recursive case

two_airlines(X, Y, C1, C2) :- one_airline(X, Z, C1), flight(Z, Y, C2), C1 != C2. % The moment when the path becomes "two_airlines"

two_airlines(X, Y, C1, C2) :- two_airlines(X, Z, C1, C2), flight(Z, Y, C1).
two_airlines(X, Y, C1, C2) :- two_airlines(X, Z, C1, C2), flight(Z, Y, C2). % Both airlines are allowed!

ans(X, Y) :- two_airlines(X, Y, _, _).
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{note}{}
    Two variables \code{A} and \code{B} (so with different names) \emph{can} be bound to the same constant. By writing two variables with distinct names, we are essentially telling Datalog that those two variables are allowed to be bound to different constants.
    To impose different bindings, one should add the condition \code{A != B} to the rule body.
\end{note}

or \q{find the couples of airports X, Y s.t. one can reach Y from X with paths of an even number of flights s.t. all the flights are operated by the same airline or the flights are operated by two different airlines s.t. the odd flights are operated by one airline and even ones by the other airline}:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=prolog]
% The idea is to consider a path of length two as the base case. In this way, by concatenating paths of length two, we will obtain only even paths "by design"
even_one_airline(X, Y, C) :- flight(X, Z, C), flight(Z, Y, C).
even_one_airline(X, Y, C) :- even_one_airline(X, Z, C), even_one_airline(Z, Y, C).

even_two_airlines(X, Y, C1, C2) :- flight(X, Z, C1), flight(Z, Y, C2), C1 != C2.
even_two_airlines(X, Y, C1, C2) :- even_two_airlines(X, Z, C1, C2), even_two_airlines(Z, Y, C1, C2).

ans(X, Y) :- even_one_airline(X, Y, _).
ans(X, Y) :- even_two_airlines(X, Y, _, _).
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{note}{}
    When several \code{ans} are specified, their results are gathered.
\end{note}

or \q{find the couples of airports X, Y s.t. one can reach Y from X with flights operated by two companies at most}:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=prolog]
% The idea is again to save the airlines inside the newly generated facts:
% at_most_two_airlines(X, Y, C1, C2) :- flight(X, Y, C1).
% Wait! The previous rule is unsafe: we need a gimmick to make it safe: we add the airline predicate
airline(X) :- flight(_, _, X).

at_most_two_airlines(X, Y, C1, C2) :- flight(X, Y, C1), airline(C2). % C1 and C2 are allowed to be the same
at_most_two_airlines(X, Y, C1, C2) :- at_most_two_airlines(X, Z, C1, C2), flight(Z, Y, C1).
at_most_two_airlines(X, Y, C1, C2) :- at_most_two_airlines(X, Z, C1, C2), flight(Z, Y, C2). % Both airlines are allowed!

ans(X, Y) :- at_most_two_airlines(X, Y, _, _).
\end{lstlisting}

We \emph{can} express all of these queries about paths since adding a new fact would not invalidate the already found paths.
